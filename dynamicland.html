dynamicland.org digested

Site digest · 17 Nov 2026 · Source: https://dynamicland.org/

Dynamicland is a nonprofit research lab building what it calls a humane dynamic medium: computing that lives in the real world, on ordinary physical materials, with people actually together in the same room. It is not a product and never will be; it is a practice that is learned and taught. My reading: the site is a ten-year lab notebook for one bet, that computing can be a public good like a library rather than a device you buy.

The short version

  • What: A nonprofit research lab (Dynamicland Foundation, 501(c)(3)) and its computing system, Realtalk
  • Lineage: Founded 2014 as the Communications Design Group; the Oakland community space ran 2017 to COVID, lease ended Dec 2021
  • Now: A communal science lab with UC San Francisco, and a dynamic library under development in Berkeley
  • Berkeley: Not yet open to the public; “We hope to gradually open in 2027.”
  • Product status: None. “You can’t buy this. You can’t download this. You’ll never be able to buy this.”
  • Support: Donations and grants; everything documented for free on the site

The idea: Three commitments

Computing should happen in the real world, with real materials and real people together, and everyone present should be able to author it.

  • In the real world. No screens, no headsets. Computation is projected onto paper and ordinary objects; you work with your hands, at human scale, in a place.
  • A communal computer. One computer per place, not one per person. Everything is out and visible, so people gather around, point, reach in, and learn from each other. The 2018 talk’s slogan: “A communal computer. Agency, not apps. Thinking like a whole human.”
  • An authoring medium. There are no apps and no users. Everybody makes things, and anything can be reprogrammed by anyone at any time, from a two-line card to the operating system itself.

How Realtalk works

  1. Programs are paper - Code is printed on physical pages. A page is running when it is out on a table, visible to everyone, and can be picked up, moved, or read by anyone.
  2. Pages talk in claims and wishes - One page claims “the fox is out”; another, seeing that claim, wishes to be labeled and outlined; a third knows how to draw the label. Small, simple pages combine into rich behavior.
  3. Edit anything, any time - Point a keyboard at any page and change its code; the change takes effect immediately, for everyone. That extends down to the compiler itself, which sits on a whiteboard.
  4. The OS is furniture - At the Oakland space the operating system lived on a bulletin board, then a poster gallery, then a binder for travel. Every line of code physically exists somewhere you can put your hands on it.
  5. Organization is free - Shelves, walls, boxes, pockets, marker doodles: arranging, grouping, and annotating programs takes zero lines of code, because the objects are real.
  6. Books are zero-code media - A book is a hole punch and a binder; pages can hold video, simulations, or other programs. “Every book in Realtalk says, you can add to me.”
  7. Small software suffices - The 2024 talk’s presentation software is 25 lines of code, made on the spot. Their comparison: “Microsoft PowerPoint is maybe 20 million lines of code. You don’t need it.”
  8. It hosts itself - The lab does its daily work, including building the next system, inside Realtalk. Laptops are for browsing the web, not for the real work.

Why not AR, voice, apps, or AI

The FAQ answers each mainstream future of computing directly. Their answers, in their terms:

  1. Not headsets (AR/VR) - A headset is a private window that isolates each person. They want the medium to be the shared place itself, not a screen glued to your face.
  2. Not voice assistants - Conversations with furniture give up everything visual and tactile; they call it a regression to an oral culture that cannot show you anything.
  3. Not personal devices - A device per person puts everyone in their own world, and those worlds are mass-manufactured by app developers who decide what you can and cannot do.
  4. Not AI as the interface - Their position: systems should be visible top to bottom; “to maximize agency, minimize what the computer knows”; people should talk to people, not tools; generative AI is “outsourcing understanding”; and models must reflect reality through explicit inference, not plausible-looking output.

Context, not gospel. These are the site authors’ stated positions from the FAQ and talks, summarized and partly quoted. Read the FAQ’s full answers before arguing with them.

The arc: 2013 to now

  1. 2013-2014: Seeing Spaces and the founding agenda - Bret Victor’s Seeing Spaces talk and the CDG research agenda set the vision: rooms for seeing and understanding systems. The young lab built screen tools (Apparatus, Earth Primer) before leaving the screen.
  2. 2015: Hypercard in the World - A laser pointer “blesses” physical objects as programmable. Posters become searchable, a book can be seen whole while you read it: the first taste of computation painted onto the room.
  3. 2017: Realtalk and the Oakland space - The Realtalk system works well enough to build a place around it: Dynamicland, a community space in Oakland. The zine is its founding document.
  4. 2018: The community takes over - The Harvard talk is mostly other people’s projects: sound toolkits, DNA models on the table, family-history maps, games live-coded while being played.
  5. 2019: Bootstrapping Research - The Stripe talk places the lab in the Engelbart and PARC lineage: research that builds its own world and lives in the future it wants for everyone.
  6. 2020: COVID and the rewrite - The space empties; the four researchers work from home over “dynalamps” and rebuild the system as Realtalk-2020, around extreme visibility. (2020 letter)
  7. 2021: A foundation, and the end of the space - The Dynamicland Foundation becomes a 501(c)(3) public charity. The Oakland lease ends in December; the beloved room is packed up. (2021 letter)
  8. 2022: Into the science lab - Realtalk-in-a-binder moves into a molecular biology lab at UC San Francisco. The website itself is now organized and generated in Realtalk. (2022 report)
  9. 2024: Back in public - A new Intro and FAQ, the communal science lab paper (a two-year grant begins that July), and the Computational Public Space talk.
  10. Now: The Berkeley dynamic library - The next space, opening progressively: a working lab first, then a communal computer for Berkeley, then a public dynamic library. The FAQ’s hope: “We hope to gradually open in 2027.”

Bootstrapping: living in the future

Research plants seeds that become industries decades later. The for loop was, in their phrase, Rutishauser’s gift to humanity, and no one pays him per loop. Their argument: today’s funding no longer plants those seeds, so the future’s foundations are not being built.

Bootstrapping, Engelbart’s term, means not making a product but building a small world with qualitatively different tools, and living in it. Xerox PARC’s Chuck Thacker: “we were spending a lot of money to simulate the future.” The laboratory itself is the experiment.

Such worlds look silly from outside. A visiting Xerox executive, shown the graphical interface, called it “a hell of an expensive Etch-a-Sketch toy for his grandchildren”. Their rule of thumb: if it doesn’t look a little silly, it’s probably incremental.

The 2019 talk’s sharpest example is the pop-up menu. In Smalltalk it was educational scaffolding, teaching children the vocabulary to go beyond the menu; its commercial descendants are access to functionality, teaching dependence on a corporation. “This amazing idea that the purpose of a user interface is to teach you to go beyond the interface, that idea has been buried.”

How the talk ends: these what-ifs aren’t hypothetical. “They’re just how we do our work. And we hope that in however many decades, everybody in the world will be able to live like this as well.”

A community space as research method

Thousands of people came through the Oakland space; the most fruitful work came from hundreds of residents: students, teachers, scientists, artists, activists doing their own projects in the new medium.

Spontaneous collaboration is the designed default: because everything is visible, people walk by, sit down, and join in. Game jams produced what they call a communally-authored stew of software, where nobody even remembered who made what, because everybody made everything.

Two scenes from the 2019 talk: a Mario-style game played, programmed, drawn, and level-designed all at the same time by different people; and UCSF grad students who, after an hour of live-coding a DNA environment together, wished they could work there instead of in the commercial tool they paid for.

Children start by playing with things left lying around, then tweak, then make their own. The gentle slope from play to authorship is the curriculum. The spread model is kits, games, and teaching, so other communities can grow their own, not units shipped.

The communal science lab

Since 2022 the lab’s deep collaboration is with Shawn Douglas’s molecular biology lab at UC San Francisco; Douglas created Cadnano, the standard design tool for DNA origami. In July 2024 a two-year grant began to integrate Realtalk into the lab’s work.

The founding paper depicts a communal science lab: scientists editing proteins on the table, pinning analysis programs to the wall, working the wet lab with computation present in the room instead of locked in personal screens. “Improvising cellular playgrounds in Realtalk” was the first scientific presentation delivered in Realtalk itself.

Computational Public Space (Nov 2024)

At the Screenless Cities Conference: a values-driven framework for integrating computation into cities, borrowing the vocabulary of urban design. Nothing built for cities yet; by his own account, an invitation to start that conversation. (talk text)

  1. Local culture vs globalization - There is a McDonald’s on every corner and an Instagram on every phone. A map of a neighborhood, he argues, should be made by the neighborhood. Computing as a local practice, not a mass-market product.
  2. Participatory vs centrally planned - Robert Moses remade New York top-down; Google Docs is the same, a committee’s world you accept as-is. The alternative: environments the community can reprogram.
  3. Communal vs isolating - Suburban houses and laptops both keep neighbors strangers. Technology can instead be structured so it naturally brings people into contact.
  4. Public space vs private property - Twitter feels like a public square but is privately owned. Computational public space, he argues, requires computation in actual space, publicly held, not private apps installed in public.
  5. Privacy vs surveillance - Once life happens inside screens, everything becomes data and privacy law is, in his view, a lost cause. The counter: a computer that never collects. “The computer that I’m using to give this talk right now doesn’t even know my name.”
  6. Awareness vs consumption - Cities offer shopping and entertainment; libraries and museums hold depth but sit apart from daily life. Bring the knowledge out into public space, where learning happens by immersion.
  7. Visible vs hidden - Water, power, transit, supply chains, taxes, climate: civilization runs on systems nobody can see. Computation in public space could make them visible at their touchpoints. “You can’t build a civilized society on magic and superstition.”

The Library (2019), and the Berkeley plan

The Library is a short fiction, and the site calls it Bret’s vision of a dynamic library. You enter a six-story cylindrical building where every domain of knowledge presents a living model bigger than you are, and the map in your hands is a map of the knowable. Its messages: the world is knowable, and knowledge is constructed, not received.

The Berkeley project is that vision made concrete, opening progressively: a working lab first, then a communal computer for Berkeley, then a public dynamic library. The FAQ’s hope is a gradual opening in 2027.

From worrydream to Dynamicland

Dynamicland is the continuation of the twenty-year arc documented on worrydream.com (digested separately in this repo): the essays about representations and media became a lab, then a room, and now a would-be public institution.
